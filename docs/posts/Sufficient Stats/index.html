6.1 Introduction

We use information from a sample \(X_1 … X_n\) to make inferences about a population parameter \(\theta\).

Often, \(n\) is too large and we can’t make sense of it without summarizing.

Some common ways to summarize are, calculating the mean, variance, max, min etc.

Notation:

\(\underset{\sim}{X}\) is a random vector denoting the random variables \(X_1 … X_n\).

\(\times\) denotes an observed vector of \(x_1 .. x_n\)

\(\chi\) sample space of all possible samples \(X\)

\(\mathcal{T}\) all possible values of \(T(.)\) over \(\chi\)

Definition of a statistic

\(T(\underset{\sim}{X})\) a statistic is any form of data reduction.

Suppose we have two observed samples \(x\) ang \(y\) and we discard them after converting to \(T(x)\) and \(T(y)\). If \(T(x) = T(y)\) then both samples are treated as the same to the experimenter.

The goal of the chapter is to study methods that discard information from the sample such that as much useful information about \(\theta\) is retained.

6.2 The Sufficiency Principle

::: callout-note

## Sufficient Statistic

A statistic \(T(\underset{\sim}{X})\)is sufficient for \(\theta\) if \(P(\underset{\sim}{X} | T(X))\) does not depend on \(\theta\)
